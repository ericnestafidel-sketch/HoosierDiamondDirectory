How to Break In a Baseball Glove (the Right Way)

A new glove is stiff on purpose — thick leather holds its shape for years, but it starts out too firm to close around a ball. Breaking it in is slow and boring on purpose: a thin coat of conditioner, steady pocket work, and, above all, playing catch until the leather molds to your hand. The shortcuts parents reach for — the oven, the microwave, soaking it — are the fastest ways to wreck a glove. Glove sizing guide · Best glove for 10U

The short answer: play catch with it, 15–20 minutes a few days a week, and store it with a ball wrapped in the pocket overnight. Most youth gloves are game-ready in a few days of that. A stiff pro-grade glove takes two to three weeks of daily catch — sometimes a full season if catch is all you do.

Method 1: Play catch — the gold standard

If you only trust one method, make it this one. Playing catch with a regulation baseball is the best way to break in any glove, because real catches shape the pocket to your hand — no shortcut shapes it the same way. Play catch for 15–20 minutes, a few days a week, catching the ball in the same pocket spot each time. After each session, squeeze the glove closed around the ball and leave it wrapped so the pocket holds its shape. If a young player is doing the breaking in, a parent or coach should supervise — mostly so nobody reaches for one of the risky shortcuts below.

Method 2: Condition, wrap, then catch — the Rawlings way

Rawlings publishes a five-step break-in guide, and it's the gentlest method of the big brands, which is why it's the default for a kid's glove:

  1. Work the padding. Grab the heel of the glove with both hands and twist the thumb and pinky down and in, so the top of the pinky touches the bottom of the thumb, then the other way. The two hinges near the heel are where most gloves are stiffest — and this is the step a small kid physically can't do, so it's yours.
  2. Soften the leather, lightly. A light coating of glove oil or conditioner. Rawlings is blunt: "Do not saturate the glove with oil" — the leather should still offer resistance when you push and pull on it. A little goes a long way.
  3. Start the pocket. Put a baseball in the pocket, fold the glove closed, wrap it with rubber bands, and let it rest in a cool, dry place for a day or so. Then let the kid play hand catch, tossing a ball into the pocket and closing the glove around it.
  4. Play catch. Over and over. Rawlings' own words: "The best way to get your glove in playing shape is to play with it." The oils from the hand soften the lining and the impact of the ball shapes the pocket.
  5. Maintain it. Store it in a cool, dry place with a ball in the pocket, check the laces every six months, and give it a light treatment after every season or so.

Method 3: Warm water and a mallet — the Wilson Aso way

When time is short, many players use the method popularized by Shigeaki Aso, Wilson's longtime glove craftsman. Pour a small amount of warm — never hot or boiling — water into the pocket and palm area only, keeping it out of the finger stalls, where trapped moisture is harder to dry and can lead to mildew. Then work the leather by hand, stretch the thumb and pinky sections, and pound the pocket with a glove mallet to form it, finishing by wrapping the glove around a ball overnight. It's faster than catch alone, but it takes more care — best handled by an adult or an experienced player, especially on an expensive glove.

The brands disagree on water and heat — follow the maker of your glove. Rawlings says it plainly: "We do not recommend steaming, boiling, baking, or otherwise heating up your glove." Wilson's method starts with warm water. Both end the same way — play catch. If your glove is a Rawlings, use the Rawlings method; if it's a Wilson, the Wilson method is the one its maker stands behind.

How long does it actually take?

Glove typeHow long
Youth glove under ~$100 (Rawlings Select Pro Lite, Wilson A500, Mizuno Prospect)A few days of catch
Mid-grade leather, regular catch1–2 weeks
Pro-grade leather (Heart of the Hide, Wilson A2000)2–3 weeks of daily catch
Pro-grade leather, catch only, no mallet or steamUp to a full season

Many factory-broken-in youth gloves (Select Pro Lite, Sure Catch) arrive about 90% game-ready and need a few days of catch, not a full break-in. If an 8-year-old's glove is still too stiff to close after a week of catch, it's too big a glove, not an under-broken one.

What NOT to do

ShortcutWhy it wrecks the glove
Oven or microwaveHeat dries out and shrinks the leather, makes laces brittle, and can void the warranty — Rawlings warns heat "can damage your glove and impact your warranty"
Soaking it in waterWaterlogs the leather, adds weight, and invites mildew in the finger stalls
Running it over with a carCrushes the shape flat — a glove is shaped, not pressed
A tub of shaving creamSaturates the leather; a little conditioner is the whole point, a soak is the opposite of it
Petroleum products (Vaseline, motor oil)Breaks down leather fibers instead of conditioning them
Over-oilingSoftens the leather past the point where it holds a pocket — one thin coat, then stop
